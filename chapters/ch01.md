#! Part A | Getting through the screen

# How Quant Developer Hiring Works

Before learning anything technical, you need an accurate picture of what you are preparing for. Most candidates who fail QD screens do not fail because they lacked ability; they fail because they prepared for the wrong test, ran out of time on the online assessment, or could not explain work they had actually done. This chapter describes the hiring process as it typically works across HFT and quantitative trading firms, what each stage is designed to detect, and how to prepare for each.

> **A note on accuracy.** Firms differ, and each firm's process changes from year to year. Everything in this chapter describes *common patterns*, not any specific firm's current process. Section 1.8 explains how to get reliable, current information for the firms you actually apply to.

## What a Quant Developer Does

Job titles at trading firms are not standardized, so start with the work rather than the label. Broadly, trading firms have three overlapping technical roles:

| Role (common names) | Core work | What interviews emphasize |
|---|---|---|
| Quant Developer, Software Engineer (trading), Core Developer | Builds and optimizes the trading system: market-data handlers, order gateways, order books, strategy infrastructure, risk checks, simulation and research tooling | Algorithms, C++ depth, operating systems, concurrency, computer architecture, system design; some probability |
| Quant Researcher | Finds and models trading signals from data | Probability, statistics, linear algebra, machine learning, research reasoning; some coding |
| Quant Trader | Runs and adjusts strategies, manages risk, makes decisions under uncertainty | Probability, mental math, expected-value games, betting and market-making exercises |

The boundaries are blurry. At some firms "quant developer" means a developer embedded with researchers who mainly writes research and data tooling (often in Python and C++); at others it means a latency-focused C++ engineer working on the production trading path. The job description and conversations with people who have done the internship are the only reliable guides. This book prepares you for the more demanding end: the latency-focused C++ systems role. If you can pass those interviews, you can pass the research-tooling ones.

### What "low latency" means for the work

In a latency-sensitive trading system, the time between a market event arriving at the network card and the firm's order leaving it (*tick-to-trade*, Chapter 17) directly affects whether an opportunity is captured. That changes everyday engineering decisions: memory allocation, locking, system calls, cache misses and branch mispredictions on the critical path become things you measure and remove. Interviewers therefore probe whether you understand *what the machine actually does* when your code runs, not just whether the code is correct. Most of Parts B and C exist for this reason.

## The Pipeline

A typical internship hiring process has four or five stages. Not every firm uses every stage, and the order sometimes varies.

| Stage | Typical form | What it is designed to detect | Where this book helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| Résumé shortlist | Résumé (plus, on campus, academic record and sometimes a short form) screened against role requirements | Evidence of strong problem solving and relevant depth: competitive programming, systems projects, relevant courses | Section 1.3; the capstone (Ch 19) |
| Online assessment (OA) | Timed test: coding problems, often with multiple-choice sections on math, probability, C/C++, operating systems or computer architecture | Speed and correctness under time pressure; breadth of fundamentals | Ch 2–4, Ch 5–11 |
| Technical interviews | Usually two to four rounds, 45–60 minutes each, with a shared editor or whiteboard | Depth: can you reason about algorithms, C++, systems, probability out loud and handle hints | Ch 2–18, Ch 20 |
| Final or fit round | Conversation with a senior engineer or manager; sometimes another technical round | Motivation, communication, how you work with others, whether you understand the role | Ch 20 |
| Offer | Internship offer; the internship itself then functions as the long interview for a pre-placement offer (PPO) | — | Ch 21 |

Campus hiring usually compresses this: the shortlist, OA and interviews can happen within days or weeks, and interviews on a placement day can run back to back. The practical consequence is that **you cannot start preparing once you are shortlisted.** Everything in Part A should be solid before the OA season begins.

## The Résumé Shortlist

The résumé screen is the stage you have the most control over and the one candidates neglect most. Reviewers spend little time on each résumé, so the résumé must make your strongest evidence obvious within a few lines.

### What reviewers look for

- **Evidence of algorithmic strength.** Competitive programming ratings (Codeforces, CodeChef, AtCoder), olympiad results, contest rankings. A rating is a compact, hard-to-fake signal, which is why it is valued.
- **Evidence of systems depth.** Projects where you had to understand the machine: a memory allocator, a lock-free queue, an order book with measured latency, an operating system or compiler course project, a network protocol implementation. The capstone in Chapter 19 is designed to be such a project.
- **Relevant coursework.** Data structures and algorithms, operating systems, computer architecture, computer networks, compilers, probability and statistics.
- **Academic record.** Many firms use grades as a filter for campus hiring. You cannot change past grades quickly, but you can make sure the rest of the résumé is strong.

### Writing project bullets that survive an interview

Every line on your résumé is an invitation for an interviewer to dig in. The test for each bullet is: *could I talk about this for ten minutes, including what I would do differently?* A good bullet states what you built, the technical decisions that mattered, and a result you actually measured.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| Built a high-performance order book in C++. | Implemented a price-level order book in C++20 (array-indexed levels, intrusive order lists, no allocation on the update path); measured median update latency with `rdtsc` over a 10M-message synthetic feed. |
| Worked on multithreading in a project. | Replaced a mutex-protected queue between parser and book threads with a single-producer single-consumer ring buffer; verified with ThreadSanitizer; reported throughput and p99 latency before and after. |
| Knowledge of C++, Python, Linux. | (Remove. Skills are demonstrated by the project bullets; a bare list adds little and invites questions you may not want.) |

Two rules matter more than wording:

- **Never claim a number you did not measure, or a technology you cannot discuss.** Interviewers at these firms routinely ask how a number was measured, on what hardware, and with what methodology. A vague or invented number is worse than no number.
- **Keep it to one page,** with the strongest evidence in the top third.

## The Online Assessment

The OA is the stage that eliminates the most candidates. It is usually taken on a commercial assessment platform, under a strict time limit, and is scored automatically.

### Common components

- **Coding problems.** Typically two to four problems in 60–120 minutes, scored by hidden test cases. Difficulty is usually comparable to LeetCode medium-to-hard or Codeforces Div. 2 B–D problems, though this varies widely. Partial credit for passing some test cases is common.
- **Multiple-choice sections.** Probability and quantitative aptitude, C/C++ semantics ("what does this print?"), operating systems, computer architecture, sometimes data structures. Some tests apply negative marking for wrong answers; read the instructions.
- **Debugging or code-reading tasks.** Occasionally: find the bug in a snippet, or predict output.

### How the score is actually earned

Hidden test cases check three things: correctness on ordinary inputs, correctness on edge cases (empty input, a single element, maximum values, duplicates, negative numbers), and speed on the largest inputs. A solution that is correct but has the wrong complexity passes the small tests and times out on the large ones. The skill being measured is not "can you eventually solve this" but "can you find an approach with the right complexity, implement it without bugs, and handle edge cases, inside the time limit."

### Strategy

1. **Read every problem first** (about five minutes). Rank them by how quickly you expect to solve them, not by the order given.
2. **Solve in order of expected points per minute.** A fully correct easy problem beats a half-finished hard one.
3. **Estimate complexity before coding.** From the input limits, decide what complexity is acceptable (Section 3.1 gives the rule of thumb) and only then choose the approach.
4. **Write from a tested template.** Fast input/output, `long long` where sums can exceed about 2×10^{9}, and your standard helpers. Section 3.2 gives a template.
5. **Test edge cases before submitting.** Spend two minutes on the smallest and largest cases. This is the cheapest marks available.
6. **If stuck, take partial credit.** A brute force that passes small test cases is worth points; submit it, then try to improve.
7. **For multiple-choice questions with negative marking,** answer when you can eliminate enough options that the expected value of guessing is positive (Section 2.4 shows how to compute this).

### Common failure modes

| Failure | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Time limit exceeded on large tests | Wrong complexity, or slow I/O | Check limits first; use the fast I/O template |
| Wrong answer on hidden tests only | Overflow, off-by-one, unhandled edge case | `long long` by default for sums and products; explicit edge-case checklist |
| Never reached the last problem | Got stuck on one problem | Time-box each problem; move on and return |
| Lost marks on MCQs | Guessing under negative marking, or C++ semantics gaps | Expected-value rule for guessing; Part B for semantics |

## Technical Interview Rounds

Interview rounds test depth, communication and how you handle not knowing something. They are usually conducted in a shared editor; some interviewers ask you to compile and run, others only to write code that would compile.

| Round type | Typical content | How to prepare | Chapters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Algorithms and data structures | One or two problems; you discuss approaches, complexity, then implement and test | Pattern practice plus explaining aloud; practise without an IDE | 3, 4, 20 |
| C++ deep-dive | Object layout, virtual dispatch, move semantics, templates, STL internals, undefined behaviour, "what does this code do and why" | Understand mechanisms, not just syntax; read assembly for small examples | 5–11 |
| Systems | Caches, memory, virtual memory, scheduling, system calls, concurrency, memory orderings, networking | Be able to explain each mechanism and its cost | 12–16 |
| Probability and puzzles | Expected value, conditional probability, Markov chains, classic puzzles, mental math | Learn the methods, then drill speed | 2 |
| Design | Design a component: order book, market-data handler, rate limiter, logger; discuss data structures, threading and latency | Practise structured walkthroughs | 17–19 |
| Project discussion | Deep questions on your résumé projects | Know every decision and measurement; be able to say what you would change | 19, 21 |

### What interviewers evaluate

Interviewers are usually asked to assess a small number of things, and it helps to know what they are:

- **Problem solving.** Did you find a correct approach, and how much help did you need?
- **Technical depth.** When pushed one level deeper ("why is that slow?", "what does the compiler do here?"), do you have real answers?
- **Code quality.** Correct, readable, handles edge cases, sensible names.
- **Communication.** Can the interviewer follow your reasoning? Do you clarify ambiguous requirements before coding?
- **Response to hints and mistakes.** Do you use hints well? When you find a bug, do you fix it calmly?
- **Honesty about limits.** Saying "I don't know, but here is how I would reason about it" is a positive signal. Bluffing is usually detected and is a strong negative signal.

Chapter 20 develops all of this into a concrete method with mock interview sets.

## A Preparation Plan

The plan below assumes about 3–4 focused hours on weekdays and more at weekends alongside coursework. Adjust the pace, but keep the order: Part A first, because it gets you through the screen.

### Twelve-week plan

| Weeks | Focus | Chapters | Daily routine |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Process, diagnostic test, probability foundations | 1, 2.1–2.4 | Probability problems; 1 timed coding problem |
| 2 | Probability (distributions, Markov chains, walks, puzzles) and mental math | 2 | 15 min mental-math drill; probability problem set |
| 3–4 | Algorithms I: complexity, arrays, hashing, two pointers, binary search, stacks, heaps, greedy | 3 | 3 problems per day, one timed |
| 5–6 | Algorithms II: trees, graphs, dynamic programming, segment and Fenwick trees, strings | 4 | 3 problems per day; one full timed OA simulation per week |
| 7 | C++ foundations, object model, memory management | 5, 6, 7 | Read, compile and modify each listing |
| 8 | Modern C++, templates, STL internals, undefined behaviour | 8–11 | Listings; 10 "what does this print" questions |
| 9 | Architecture and Linux | 12, 13 | Listings plus measurement exercises |
| 10 | Concurrency and networking | 14, 15 | Build the SPSC queue yourself |
| 11 | Compilers and profiling; microstructure; component design | 16–18 | Assembly reading; design walkthroughs out loud |
| 12 | Capstone, mock interviews | 19, 20 | Mock interviews with a friend; revise Key Takeaways pages |

Throughout all twelve weeks: one timed coding problem per day keeps your OA speed from decaying, and a short mental-math drill keeps arithmetic fast.

### Four-week compressed plan

If the OA is a month away, cut to what the screen tests:

| Week | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1 | Chapter 2 (probability), skimming the code in 2.11–2.12; daily timed coding problems from Chapter 3's list |
| 2 | Chapter 3 fully; two timed OA simulations |
| 3 | Chapter 4; C++ "what does this print" and OS/architecture MCQ basics: Key Takeaways pages of Chapters 5–13 |
| 4 | Timed OA simulations every other day; review mistakes; Chapter 20 |

Then return to Parts B–D before interviews.

### How to practise effectively

- **Time yourself.** Untimed practice builds understanding; timed practice builds the skill the OA measures. You need both.
- **Keep an error log.** For every wrong answer, record the cause in one line (overflow, misread constraint, wrong recurrence, forgot an edge case). Review it weekly. Most people repeat a small number of mistake types.
- **Re-solve, don't re-read.** A week after solving a problem, solve it again from scratch without notes. If you cannot, you memorized rather than understood.
- **Explain aloud.** Interview performance depends on reasoning out loud. Practise by explaining solutions to a friend or recording yourself.

## Getting Accurate Information About Specific Firms

Because processes vary and change, you need current, firm-specific information. In order of reliability:

1. **The job description and the firm's own careers pages.** They describe the role and sometimes the process.
2. **Seniors who interned at the firm recently,** ideally in the last one or two cycles. Ask concrete questions: how many rounds, what topics came up, what language, whether code had to compile, what they wish they had prepared.
3. **Your institute's placement office,** which often has notes from previous cycles and knows the schedule.
4. **Public interview reports and forums.** Useful for topic coverage, but treat individual anecdotes as noisy: they are self-reported, often from different roles and years, and skewed toward unusual experiences.

Do not over-fit to rumoured "question banks". Questions change, and interviewers vary them deliberately. Mastery of the underlying topics is the only preparation that transfers.

## *Practice: Diagnostic Test

Take this ten-question test before starting Chapter 2, without looking anything up, in about 15 minutes. It is not a measure of ability; it tells you which chapters to prioritize. Answers and the routing table are in Appendix A (Section A.1).

**D1.** What is the time complexity of building a binary heap from *n* unsorted elements using the standard bottom-up method?

**D2.** You roll a fair six-sided die until it shows a 6. What is the expected number of rolls?

**D3.** What is the probability of at least one 6 in four rolls of a fair die? An approximate decimal is enough.

**D4.** On a typical x86-64 Linux system, what does this print, and why?

```cpp
#include <cstdio>
struct S { char a; int b; char c; };
int main() { std::printf("%zu\n", sizeof(S)); }
```

**D5.** A `std::vector<int>` has `size() == capacity()`. You call `push_back`. What happens to existing iterators and references into the vector?

**D6.** Is signed integer overflow (e.g., `INT_MAX + 1` on an `int`) defined behaviour in C++?

**D7.** What does `std::move(x)` actually do at runtime?

**D8.** What is the typical cache-line size on current x86-64 CPUs?

**D9.** Exchanges commonly distribute market data to many subscribers using which transport: TCP unicast or UDP multicast? Give one reason.

**D10.** In the C++ memory model, a store with `memory_order_release` is meant to pair with what on the reading thread, and what does the pair guarantee?

## *Key Takeaways

- QD roles vary between firms; this book prepares you for the latency-focused C++ systems end, which subsumes the rest.
- The usual pipeline is résumé shortlist, online assessment, two to four technical rounds, and a final round. Campus timelines compress it, so prepare Part A before OA season begins.
- The résumé is a set of invitations to dig deeper. Every bullet must survive ten minutes of questioning; never claim numbers you did not measure.
- The OA rewards picking the right complexity from the input limits, bug-free implementation, edge-case testing, and solving problems in order of points per minute.
- Interviews evaluate problem solving, depth when pushed one level further, code quality, communication, response to hints, and honesty about what you do not know.
- Prepare in order: probability and algorithms first, then C++ depth, then systems, then trading-domain design. Practise timed, keep an error log, re-solve rather than re-read, and explain aloud.
- Get firm-specific facts from recent interns and official sources; treat forum anecdotes as noisy.
