# Networking

Every trade starts and ends as a packet. Market data arrives as UDP datagrams, orders leave over TCP connections, and the time between the two, *tick-to-trade* (17.6), is dominated more often by how bytes cross the network and the operating system than by the strategy's arithmetic. An interviewer who asks "why is market data sent over multicast?" (diagnostic D9) or "what happens between the wire and `recvfrom` returning?" wants to see you follow a packet through every layer and say what each layer costs.

This chapter covers the two transports and their pathologies (15.1), multicast feeds with sequence numbers, A/B arbitration and gap detection (15.2), the Linux receive path and where it loses time and packets (15.3), kernel bypass (15.4), and timestamping, clock synchronization and FPGAs (15.5). The showdown (15.6) takes a feed receiver from the version most people write first to one that is five times faster at the median and stops losing packets in bursts. Feed *protocols* and recovery procedures are covered in 17.4, binary parsing in 18.2.

**About the measurements.** Listings were compiled with g++ 13.3 and run in the build container (a KVM guest on a 2.1 GHz Xeon, 2 vCPUs, Linux 6.18). The container has no physical NIC, so every network listing uses the **loopback interface**: the kernel's socket, UDP, IP and multicast code all run, but the "wire" is a function call. Loopback numbers show the kernel's share of the cost, not a NIC's or a switch's; typical ranges for real hardware are given separately and labelled. The kernel-bypass listing (15.4) needs a real interface and privileges; it is compiled by the build but not run.

## TCP vs UDP

### What is on the wire

A UDP datagram over IPv4 and Ethernet carries a 14-byte Ethernet header (destination MAC, source MAC, EtherType), a 20-byte IPv4 header, an 8-byte UDP header (ports, length, checksum), the payload and a 4-byte frame check sequence (FCS, a CRC-32). Each frame is also preceded by 8 bytes of preamble and start delimiter and followed by at least 12 byte-times of inter-frame gap, and the minimum frame is 64 bytes including the FCS, so tiny payloads are padded.

The time to put a frame on the wire is its **serialization delay**, bits divided by line rate. A minimum frame occupies 64 + 20 = 84 byte-times: 67.2 ns at 10 Gb/s (at most 14.88 million frames per second) and 26.9 ns at 25 Gb/s; a full 1518-byte frame takes 1.23 µs at 10 Gb/s. It is paid again at every store-and-forward hop. Then there is **propagation**: light in optical fibre travels at about c/1.47, roughly 4.9 µs per kilometre; radio through air is close to c, about 3.34 µs per kilometre (Problem P15.6).

### UDP and TCP

**UDP** adds ports and a checksum to IP and nothing more. Each `sendto` produces one datagram and each `recvfrom` returns exactly one, so **message boundaries are preserved**; there is no connection, acknowledgement, retransmission, ordering or flow control. A datagram arrives once or not at all, the sender's cost does not depend on who is listening, and the receiver's kernel simply drops what does not fit (15.3). These missing features are why market data uses UDP: a feed must reach many receivers at once, a retransmission for one slow receiver must never delay the others, and stale data is worth little, so the *receiver* should decide what to do about a loss.

**TCP** gives each connection an ordered, reliable **byte stream**: every byte is numbered, acknowledged and retransmitted if lost; a receive window provides flow control and congestion control (CUBIC by default on Linux) limits data in flight. Three consequences matter for trading:

- **There are no messages.** Two 40-byte `write`s may arrive as one 80-byte read, or as reads of 1 and 79 bytes. The application frames messages, usually with a length prefix, and reassembles them across reads (`read_full` in Listing 15.1, lines 16–24; Problem P15.9 builds a full reassembler).
- **Head-of-line blocking.** After a lost segment, every later byte waits in the receiver's kernel for the retransmission, even bytes received perfectly. Retransmission after duplicate acknowledgements takes about a round trip; by the retransmission timer, at least `TCP_RTO_MIN`, 200 ms on Linux. A low-rate order connection, where few segments follow the lost one, is exactly where the timer fires.
- **The stack decides when to send.** Nagle's algorithm and delayed acknowledgements, both on by default, can hold small segments for tens of milliseconds.

Exchanges therefore use TCP for **order entry**, where every message must arrive exactly once, in order, on an authenticated session, and for retransmission and snapshot services; and UDP multicast for **market data** (15.2). Order-entry protocols add their own sequence numbers and heartbeats on top of TCP so a session can resume after a disconnect without losing or repeating orders (17.5).

### Nagle meets delayed ACK

**Nagle's algorithm** (RFC 896) holds back new small segments while earlier data is unacknowledged, coalescing them. **Delayed acknowledgement** (RFC 1122) has the receiver wait briefly before acknowledging, hoping to piggyback the ACK on a reply. Each is sensible alone; together they stall for a timer period whenever a request is sent as two writes and the sender waits for a reply. Listing 15.1 reproduces this over TCP on loopback.

## Multicast Market Data

### How IP multicast works (diagnostic D9)

A **multicast group** is an IPv4 address in 224.0.0.0/4; 239.0.0.0/8 is administratively scoped for private use, and exchanges assign groups per feed channel. A receiver *joins* a group on an interface (`IP_ADD_MEMBERSHIP`); the kernel sends an IGMP membership report and tells the NIC to accept frames for the group's multicast MAC address. Switches with **IGMP snooping** forward the group's traffic only to ports with members; across routers, PIM builds a distribution tree. The sender transmits each packet **once**; the network replicates it at every branch. The multicast MAC is 01:00:5e followed by the low 23 bits of the group address, so 32 groups share each MAC (Problem P15.2) and a NIC can accept frames for groups nobody joined, which the IP layer then discards.

Why this is the right transport for market data, the full answer to D9:

- **Fairness and scale:** one transmission reaches every subscriber at essentially the same time, at a cost to the sender independent of the number of subscribers. A unicast fan-out reaches the first client before the last.
- **No per-receiver state:** no connections, acknowledgements or retransmissions, so a slow or lossy receiver cannot delay anyone else.
- **Loss is the receiver's problem:** every message carries a sequence number, and a receiver that sees a gap fills it from a redundant line or a recovery service. The receiver must do what TCP would have done: detect loss, order, de-duplicate, recover.

### Receiving multicast on Linux

The helpers in `code/ch15/mcast.hpp` (`mc::open_rx` and `mc::open_tx`, not reproduced) open the sockets that this chapter's listings use. Each option they set is a place where real feed handlers go wrong:

- **`SO_REUSEADDR`** lets several sockets (an A and a B handler, or several processes) bind the same group and port. Bind the *group* address, not `INADDR_ANY`, which also accepts unicast to that port.
- **`IP_MULTICAST_ALL = 0`.** On Linux this option defaults to 1: a socket bound to `INADDR_ANY:port` receives datagrams for *every group any socket on the host has joined* on that port. ## The Kernel Network Stack and Its Cost

### The receive path

Between the wire and `recvfrom` returning, a datagram passes through these steps on a Linux host with a modern NIC:

1. **NIC:** the MAC checks the FCS and the destination address, then picks a **receive queue** by *receive-side scaling* (RSS, a hash of addresses and ports) or an explicit flow-steering rule (`ethtool -N`). Each queue is a ring of descriptors pointing at buffers the driver posted in advance.
2. **DMA:** the NIC writes the frame into the next buffer across PCIe and writes back the descriptor (length, status, hash, checksum result, optionally a hardware timestamp).
3. **Interrupt:** the NIC raises the queue's MSI-X interrupt, unless *interrupt moderation* tells it to wait for more frames or a few microseconds (`ethtool -C`: `rx-usecs`, `rx-frames`, adaptive modes). The interrupt goes to the CPU its affinity names (13.6).
4. **NAPI poll:** the handler masks the queue's interrupt and schedules the driver's poll function in the `NET_RX` softirq, which processes a budget of frames per poll. Sustained softirq load is deferred to the `ksoftirqd` thread, scheduled like any other thread (13.4): a latency cliff under load.
5. **Protocols:** the driver builds an `sk_buff` (the kernel's packet descriptor); packet taps (`tcpdump`) see it; then IP with any netfilter rules and connection tracking, then UDP, which looks up the receiving socket(s). Multicast is cloned to every matching socket.
6. **Socket queue:** if the socket's accounted memory is below its receive buffer, the skb is queued and a blocked thread is woken; otherwise it is **dropped and counted** (15.3.3).
7. **Wake-up and copy:** the woken thread is scheduled, possibly on another CPU (an IPI and perhaps a C-state exit, 13.4), and `recvfrom` copies the payload to user space and frees the skb.

Transmit mirrors it: `sendto` copies the payload into a new skb, routes it (computing the multicast MAC, or resolving a unicast neighbour with ARP), passes it through the queueing discipline, and the driver writes a descriptor and rings the NIC's *doorbell*, an uncached MMIO write; the NIC fetches descriptor and data by DMA and a completion interrupt later frees the skb.

### What it costs, and the knobs

The expensive steps are the **interrupt** (and moderation, which delays it on purpose), the **wake-up** of a blocked thread, the system calls (100–300 ns each, 13.5) and **cache misses** on packet data and socket structures touched by different CPUs. On a well-tuned server a datagram typically takes a few microseconds from the NIC to a busy-polling application through the kernel; with default moderation and a blocked receiver, tens of microseconds is common. (Typical ranges; the container has no NIC to measure.) Listing 15.6 splits a loopback transfer with kernel timestamps: about 1 µs from `sendto` to the loopback "driver", 0.1 µs across the loopback "wire", and about 2 µs from the receive hand-off until the polling receiver has the data, which includes IP and UDP processing (on loopback, run in the *sender's* softirq) and moving the queued skb's cache lines to the receiver's core.

The standard tuning, roughly in order of impact:

- **Do not block.** A receiver that busy-polls a non-blocking socket never pays the wake-up (15.6). `SO_BUSY_POLL` (or `net.core.busy_read`) goes further on supporting drivers: a receive call spins in the device's NAPI poll for up to the given microseconds, short-circuiting steps 3–4.
- **Interrupts:** pin each queue's interrupt to a chosen core on the NIC's NUMA node (13.6, 13.7), reduce moderation on market-data queues (more CPU per packet, less delay), and steer the feed's flows to known queues.
- **Batch system calls** with `recvmmsg` (15.6).
- **Keep the path short:** no netfilter rules or connection tracking on market-data interfaces, no capture taps in production.
- **Size the socket buffer** for the worst stall you must survive.

## Kernel Bypass

### The idea

Kernel bypass moves steps 3–7 of 15.3 into the application. The NIC's receive and transmit rings and the packet buffers they point to are mapped into the process; the application **polls** the receive ring on a dedicated core instead of waiting for an interrupt, reads packets where the NIC wrote them, and posts transmit descriptors and rings the doorbell itself. No interrupts, system calls, sk_buffs, socket queues or wake-ups, and often no copies. What remains is the NIC, PCIe and the application's own cache misses. The main families:

- **Socket-compatible acceleration:** a library loaded with `LD_PRELOAD` intercepts socket calls and runs a user-space network stack over the vendor's NIC: OpenOnload for AMD (formerly Solarflare) NICs, VMA and the newer XLIO for NVIDIA (Mellanox) NICs. Unmodified applications benefit; unsupported operations fall back to the kernel, and corner cases (`fork`, signals, mixing accelerated and kernel sockets in one `epoll`) need care.
- **Vendor raw APIs** at the Ethernet-frame level: `ef_vi` (AMD Solarflare), `libexanic` (Cisco ExaNIC), raw-packet queue pairs through `ibverbs` (NVIDIA). The lowest software latency, at the price of doing IP and UDP yourself and lock-in to one NIC family.
- **DPDK:** open-source poll-mode drivers for many NICs; the NIC is detached from the kernel (bound to `vfio-pci`) and the application calls `rte_eth_rx_burst` to fetch packets into huge-page buffers. Designed mainly for throughput.
- **AF_XDP,** in mainline Linux since 4.18: an XDP program in the driver redirects chosen packets into a socket whose rings and buffer area (*UMEM*) are shared with user space. With driver support packets are DMAed straight into the UMEM (zero-copy). The kernel keeps the NIC, so other traffic and tools keep working.

### What bypass costs

A **core per poller**, spinning whether or not packets arrive and isolated as in 13.6. **Lost tooling:** bypassed traffic is invisible to `tcpdump`, netfilter and kernel counters unless the library provides equivalents (Onload ships its own capture tool). **Lock-in and operations:** specific NICs, drivers and firmware, huge-page reservations, privileges, and trust in a user-space network stack. **The work the kernel did:** buffer management, and with raw APIs ARP and IGMP. What it buys: commonly quoted software receive latencies through bypass stacks are around a microsecond or less from NIC to application on current hardware, against several microseconds for a tuned kernel path. Treat these as orders of magnitude; the numbers that matter are measured on your hardware with hardware timestamps (15.5).

## Timestamping and FPGAs

### Software and hardware timestamps

Timestamps measure tick-to-trade on every event (17.6), reconstruct what the system saw when it decided, and satisfy regulation: in the EU, MiFID II's RTS 25 requires firms doing high-frequency algorithmic trading to keep business clocks within 100 µs of UTC, with timestamp granularity of 1 µs or finer.

A **software timestamp** is a clock read by the kernel inside the stack: on receive when the driver hands the packet up, on transmit just before the driver gets it. It uses the system clock, but on receive it comes after interrupt moderation and softirq scheduling, so it does not say when the packet hit the wire. A **hardware timestamp** is latched by the NIC as the frame passes its MAC or PHY, from the NIC's own *PTP hardware clock* (PHC), with nanosecond resolution and no software delay. Linux exposes both through `SO_TIMESTAMPING`; hardware stamping must also be enabled on the device (`SIOCSHWTSTAMP`), and `ethtool -T` shows what an interface supports (neither of the container's interfaces has hardware stamping or a PHC). Listing 15.6 uses software stamps on loopback.

### Clock synchronization

Comparing timestamps from two machines needs synchronized clocks. **PTP** (IEEE 1588) exchanges hardware-timestamped messages: the master sends Sync at t_{1}, received at t_{2} by the slave's clock; the slave sends Delay_Req at t_{3}, received at t_{4} by the master's clock. Assuming equal delays both ways, offset = ((t_{2} − t_{1}) − (t_{4} − t_{3}))/2 and path delay = ((t_{2} − t_{1}) + (t_{4} − t_{3}))/2. Any path *asymmetry* becomes an offset error of half the asymmetry, which no timestamp exchange can detect (Problem P15.10). Switches acting as boundary or transparent clocks remove their own queueing from the exchange. On Linux, `ptp4l` disciplines the NIC's PHC from the network's grandmaster (typically GPS-disciplined) and `phc2sys` disciplines the system clock from the PHC. With hardware timestamping throughout, sub-microsecond agreement is typical; NTP over a general network typically achieves hundreds of microseconds to milliseconds.

The most trustworthy latency measurement does not involve the measured host at all: optical **taps** or switch mirror ports copy traffic to a capture device that timestamps every frame in hardware, giving wire-to-wire latency from market-data frame to order frame without perturbing the system.

### FPGAs

A **field-programmable gate array** is a chip of configurable logic, memories and high-speed transceivers whose wiring is set by a configuration bitstream. A design written in a hardware description language (Verilog, VHDL) or by high-level synthesis becomes a **pipeline** of stages, each doing a little work every clock cycle at a few hundred megahertz. There are no instructions, caches, interrupts or operating system, so latency is **deterministic**: the same number of cycles for every packet.

The decisive advantage is acting on bytes *as they arrive*. A 10GBASE-R interface delivers 64 bits per cycle of a 156.25 MHz clock, one 8-byte beat every 6.4 ns. A parser can recognize a message, extract a price from the first beats, update a book and start an order before the frame has finished arriving, while software cannot see the frame until all of it, FCS included, is in memory (Problem P15.7). Commonly cited wire-to-wire figures for FPGA trading paths are tens to low hundreds of nanoseconds, against microseconds for the best software paths. The trade-offs decide how they are used:

- **Roles:** feed handling (parsing, filtering, arbitration, book building) delivering normalized data to software over PCIe DMA; pre-trade risk checks inline on the order path (17.7); complete tick-to-trade for simple strategies whose parameters software sets.
- **Cost:** design, simulation and timing closure take far longer than software changes; complex logic and large state are hard.
- **The QD's part:** the software around the FPGA (configuration, parameter updates, DMA rings much like Listing 15.5's), its hardware timestamps, and the judgement of which logic is stable and latency-critical enough for hardware.

The network follows the same logic: **cut-through** switches forward once they have read the destination address instead of storing the whole frame; **layer-1 switches** replicate signals electrically with a few nanoseconds of delay; and between data centres, microwave and millimetre-wave links win because radio in air is about 1.47 times faster than light in fibre, over a straighter path.

## The Nanosecond Penalty

"Typical" gives rough ranges for current x86-64 servers and 10/25 GbE equipment, from first principles where the quantity is physical; "measured here" is from this chapter's listings over loopback in the virtualized container.

| Event | Typical | Measured here | Why it costs that |
|---|---|---|---|
| serialize 64 B frame, 10 / 25 GbE | 67.2 / 26.9 ns | — (computed) | 84 byte-times incl. preamble and gap |
| serialize 1518 B frame, 10 GbE | 1.23 µs | — (computed) | paid again at each store-and-forward hop |
| propagation, fibre / radio | ≈ 4.9 / 3.34 µs per km | — | light at c/1.47 in glass, ≈ c in air |
| switch hop | a few ns (layer 1) to hundreds of ns (cut-through) | — | electrical fan-out vs reading the header |
| NIC to host memory | a few hundred ns to ≈ 1 µs | — | MAC, DMA over PCIe, descriptor write-back |
| `sendto` to driver (UDP) | ≈ 0.5–1 µs | ≈ 1 µs (15.5) | system call, skb, UDP/IP, route |
| driver to polling app (UDP) | ≈ 0.5–2 µs | ≈ 2 µs (15.5) | IP/UDP receive, socket queue, `recvmsg` |
| kernel UDP, receiver busy-polls | a few µs | ≈ 3 µs median (15.6) | the two rows above |
| kernel UDP, receiver blocked | ≈ 5–20 µs; more with moderation | ≈ 15 µs median (15.6) | adds a cross-CPU wake-up |
| kernel-bypass receive | ≈ 1 µs or less | — (not run, 15.4) | no interrupt, syscall or socket queue |
| FPGA wire to wire | tens to hundreds of ns | — | pipeline acts on bytes as they arrive |
| receive-buffer charge, 100 B datagram | depends on driver and size | 832 B (15.3) | skb structure plus data buffer |
| Nagle + delayed ACK stall | ≈ 40 ms per exchange | ≈ 44 ms (15.1) | delayed-ACK timer |
| TCP timer retransmission | ≥ 200 ms (`TCP_RTO_MIN`) | — | head-of-line blocking for the stream |

Physics (serialization and distance) costs nanoseconds per byte and per metre and can only be shortened, not tuned. The kernel costs microseconds per packet, mostly in interrupts, wake-ups and system calls; bypass removes most of it. Protocol defaults (Nagle, retransmission timers, small buffers) cost milliseconds, rarely, which is why they show up in the 99.9th percentile and in incident reports rather than in benchmarks.

## Interview Questions

**Q1. Why do exchanges publish market data over UDP multicast but take orders over TCP?**
Multicast sends each packet once and the network replicates it, so subscribers receive it at about the same time and the exchange's cost does not grow with their number; with no per-client connections or retransmissions, one slow client cannot delay the others. Receivers handle loss with sequence numbers, A/B lines and recovery services. Orders are the opposite case: one counterparty, every message exactly once and in order, an authenticated session, so TCP's reliable stream plus the protocol's own sequence numbers fit.

**Q2. What is Nagle's algorithm, and when does it hurt?**
It holds small segments while earlier data is unacknowledged. With delayed ACKs, a write-write-read pattern stalls until the peer's delayed-ACK timer fires, about 40 ms on Linux. Set `TCP_NODELAY` on latency-sensitive sockets and write each message with one call.

**Q3. Your feed handler reports gaps. How do you find where packets are lost?**
Work up the path: switch port counters and NIC counters (`ethtool -S`) for the wire and rings; `/proc/net/softnet_stat` for per-CPU backlog drops; `RcvbufErrors` in `/proc/net/snmp` or `SO_RXQ_OVFL` for socket overflow. One line affected suggests the network; both suggest the host. Check the join (interface, IGMP seen by the switch), `rp_filter`, and `IP_MULTICAST_ALL`. Then fix the cause: interrupt affinity and moderation, buffer size, a faster non-blocking consumer, or bypass.

**Q4. What happens between a datagram reaching the NIC and `recvfrom` returning?**
Queue selection by RSS or a steering rule; DMA into a pre-posted buffer; a (moderated) MSI-X interrupt; NAPI poll in softirq builds an skb; IP, UDP, socket lookup; queued on the socket or dropped if the buffer is full; a blocked thread is woken and scheduled; `recvfrom` copies the payload out. The interrupt, the wake-up and cache misses dominate.

**Q5. What is kernel bypass, and what do you give up?**
The NIC's rings and buffers are mapped into the application, which polls them on a dedicated core: no interrupts, system calls, socket queues or copies per packet. Options: Onload or VMA (sockets API), `ef_vi`, `libexanic`, verbs (raw), DPDK, AF_XDP. You give up a core per poller, kernel tooling and filtering, NIC portability and some maturity, and take on buffer management and perhaps ARP/IGMP.

**Q6. How do you measure your trading system's latency accurately?**
Outside the host: taps or mirror ports into a capture device with hardware timestamps, for wire-to-wire latency. Inside: NIC hardware timestamps, plus cheap software stamps at stage boundaries (16.4). Synchronize with PTP using hardware timestamps, mind path asymmetry, and report percentiles.

**Q7. Why can an FPGA react faster than software with a ten times slower clock?**
It acts on bytes as they arrive (8 bytes per 6.4 ns at 10 GbE) instead of after the whole frame reaches memory; its pipeline has no interrupts, caches, system calls or scheduling, so every packet takes the same cycles; and it works on many fields in parallel. Its weaknesses are development cost and complex logic or large state, so software sets its parameters and does everything not latency-critical.

## Key Takeaways

- **Transports:** UDP keeps datagram boundaries and nothing else; TCP is a reliable ordered byte stream with framing left to you, head-of-line blocking and a 200 ms minimum timer retransmission. Market data uses UDP multicast, orders TCP. Set `TCP_NODELAY` and write each message with one call.
- **Wire physics:** serialization is bytes × 8 / rate plus 20 byte-times per frame (67.2 ns for a minimum frame at 10 GbE); fibre about 4.9 µs/km, radio about 3.34 µs/km.
- **Multicast (D9):** one transmission, network replication, no per-receiver state, loss handled by receivers. Join on the right interface, bind the group, turn `IP_MULTICAST_ALL` off, watch `rp_filter`.
- **Gaps:** sequence numbers on every message; arbitrate A/B by first copy; hold a hole briefly, then recover; heartbeats expose tail losses; never trust lengths from the network.
- **Kernel path:** queue, DMA, moderated interrupt, NAPI softirq, IP/UDP, socket queue, wake-up, copy. Busy-poll, batch with `recvmmsg`, pin interrupts, size buffers by true size per datagram, and watch `RcvbufErrors`, `SO_RXQ_OVFL`, `softnet_stat` and NIC counters.
- **Bypass:** the application polls NIC rings in its own memory (Onload/VMA, `ef_vi`/`libexanic`/verbs, DPDK, AF_XDP with SPSC rings as in 14.5), at the cost of a core, tooling and portability.
- **Time and hardware:** NIC hardware timestamps, PTP (asymmetry error is half the asymmetry), taps for wire-to-wire measurement; FPGAs act on bytes as they arrive in deterministic pipelines.
- **Showdown:** busy-polling, batching, a sized buffer and sequence checking took a loopback receiver from about 15 µs to 3 µs median and from silent burst losses to none.
