# Probability and Brainteasers for QD Rounds

Probability appears in almost every QD hiring process: as multiple-choice questions in online assessments, as a dedicated interview round, or as a warm-up question in a systems round. The questions are rarely deep mathematics. They test whether you can turn a word problem into a precise model quickly, compute without errors, and check your answer. This chapter teaches the small set of methods that solve most of these questions, then gives you the classic problems as worked examples of those methods.

The chapter template is adapted for this subject. "Low-level mechanics" becomes *how to set the problem up*. The code showdown (Section 2.11) compares a naive Monte Carlo simulation with a reproducible one that reports error bars, and with an exact solver; the nanosecond penalty (Section 2.12) measures what random-number generation and simulation error actually cost.

## How Probability Is Tested

### What the interviewer is watching

A probability question in an interview is a small modelling exercise. Interviewers usually care about the process as much as the final number:

- **Do you define the experiment precisely?** Most famous "paradoxes" come from an ambiguous description. Stating your assumptions ("I'll assume the host always opens a door with a goat") is part of a good answer.
- **Do you choose a method that makes the problem small?** The same question can take one line with linearity of expectation or a page with brute-force counting.
- **Do you check the answer?** Probabilities must lie in [0, 1]; expectations must lie between the smallest and largest possible values; extreme cases (p = 0, p = 1, n = 1) should give obvious answers; symmetric situations should give symmetric answers.
- **Can you compute quickly and exactly?** Give the exact form (7/16), then a decimal (about 0.44).

### A five-step method

1. **Restate** the problem, and name the random experiment and its outcomes.
2. **State assumptions** where the problem is ambiguous.
3. **Pick a method.** The rest of this chapter is organized by method: counting (2.2), conditioning (2.3), linearity and indicators (2.4), known distributions (2.5), geometry (2.6), first-step analysis and Markov chains (2.7), martingales and random walks (2.8), and the puzzle methods (2.9).
4. **Compute,** writing intermediate results so the interviewer can follow.
5. **Sanity-check** with an extreme case, a symmetry argument or a rough estimate.

## Counting

When all outcomes are equally likely, probability is counting: P(A) = (number of outcomes in A) / (total number of outcomes). The difficulty is entirely in counting correctly.

### The basic rules

- **Product rule.** If a choice is made in stages, with *a* options at the first stage and *b* at the second regardless of the first choice, there are *a* × *b* outcomes.
- **Sum rule.** If outcomes split into disjoint cases, add the counts of the cases.
- **Permutations.** Ordered selections of *k* from *n* distinct items: n!/(n − k)!. All orderings of *n* items: n!.
- **Combinations.** Unordered selections of *k* from *n*: C(n, k) = n! / (k! (n − k)!).
- **Multinomial.** Orderings of *n* items in which there are k_{1} identical items of type 1, k_{2} of type 2, and so on: n! / (k_{1}! k_{2}! ⋯ k_{m}!). Problem P2.1 uses this.

Useful identities, each with a counting proof you should be able to give:

- C(n, k) = C(n, n − k): choosing who is in is choosing who is out.
- C(n, k) = C(n − 1, k − 1) + C(n − 1, k) (Pascal): either item *n* is chosen or it is not.
- Σ_{k} C(m, k) C(n, r − k) = C(m + n, r) (Vandermonde): choose *r* people from *m* men and *n* women by summing over the number of men.

### Stars and bars

How many ways can 10 identical candies be given to 4 children? Write the candies as 10 stars and separate the children's shares with 3 bars: every arrangement of 10 stars and 3 bars in a row is one distribution. So the count is C(13, 3) = 286. In general, the number of non-negative integer solutions of x_{1} + ⋯ + x_{k} = n is C(n + k − 1, k − 1).

If every child must receive at least one candy, give each child one first and distribute the remaining 6 freely: C(6 + 3, 3) = C(9, 3) = 84. In general, positive solutions number C(n − 1, k − 1).

The standard "balls into boxes" cases are worth memorizing as a table:

| Balls | Boxes | Any number per box | Every box non-empty |
|---|---|---|---|
| distinct (n) | distinct (k) | k^{n} | Σ_{j=0..k} (−1)^{j} C(k, j) (k − j)^{n} (inclusion–exclusion) |
| identical (n) | distinct (k) | C(n + k − 1, k − 1) | C(n − 1, k − 1) |

### Complements and inclusion–exclusion

"At least one" questions are almost always easier through the complement. The probability of at least one six in four rolls of a die is 1 − (5/6)^{4} = 1 − 625/1296 = 671/1296 ≈ 0.518.

When several events overlap, inclusion–exclusion corrects the double counting:

P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) − P(A ∩ B) − P(A ∩ C) − P(B ∩ C) + P(A ∩ B ∩ C).

**Worked example: derangements.** *n* people each drop a hat into a box and each takes one back at random. What is the probability that nobody gets their own hat? Let A_{i} be the event that person *i* gets their own hat. For any set of *j* people, the probability that all of them get their own hats is (n − j)!/n!, and there are C(n, j) such sets. Inclusion–exclusion gives

P(at least one match) = Σ_{j=1..n} (−1)^{j+1} C(n, j) (n − j)!/n! = Σ_{j=1..n} (−1)^{j+1} / j!.

So P(no match) = Σ_{j=0..n} (−1)^{j}/j!, which converges very quickly to 1/e ≈ 0.3679. The number of derangements is D_{n} = n! × Σ_{j=0..n} (−1)^{j}/j!: D_{1} = 0, D_{2} = 1, D_{3} = 2, D_{4} = 9, D_{5} = 44. A good interview answer mentions that the probability is already within 0.001 of 1/e for n = 6.

### Symmetry

Many counting problems collapse when you notice that outcomes are exchangeable. In a shuffled deck, the probability that the ace of spades lies above the king of spades is 1/2, because swapping the two cards is a bijection between the two cases. The probability that a particular card is the top card of the deck is 1/52 regardless of any other condition that does not refer to positions. Symmetry arguments are short, so interviewers like them; but say explicitly which bijection or exchangeability you are using.

**Worked example: a flush.** The probability that a 5-card poker hand is all one suit (including straight flushes) is 4 × C(13, 5) / C(52, 5) = 4 × 1287 / 2,598,960 = 5148 / 2,598,960 ≈ 0.00198.

## Conditional Probability and Bayes

### Definitions

The probability of A given B is P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), defined when P(B) > 0. Rearranged, this is the multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(B) P(A | B), which is how you compute probabilities along a tree of sequential events.

Two events are **independent** if P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B). Do not confuse this with **mutually exclusive** (P(A ∩ B) = 0). Two mutually exclusive events with positive probability are never independent: knowing one occurred tells you the other did not.

The **law of total probability**: if B_{1}, …, B_{m} partition the outcomes, then P(A) = Σ_{i} P(A | B_{i}) P(B_{i}). It is the formal version of "split into cases".

### Bayes' rule and the odds form

Bayes' rule reverses a conditional:

P(B | A) = P(A | B) P(B) / P(A), with P(A) from the law of total probability.

For two hypotheses H and not-H, the **odds form** is faster to use in your head:

posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio, where the likelihood ratio is P(evidence | H) / P(evidence | not H).

**Worked example: a medical test.** A condition affects 1% of a population. A test detects it 99% of the time when present and gives a false positive 5% of the time when absent. Someone tests positive; what is the probability they have the condition? Prior odds are 1 : 99. The likelihood ratio is 0.99 / 0.05 = 19.8. Posterior odds are 19.8 : 99 = 1 : 5, so the probability is 1/6 ≈ 0.167. Most people guess far higher, because they confuse P(positive | condition) with P(condition | positive). This confusion has a name, the *prosecutor's fallacy*, and interviewers like to see that you avoid it.

**Worked example: a suspicious coin.** A jar holds 1000 coins; one has heads on both sides and the rest are fair. You draw one coin at random and flip it 10 times, getting 10 heads. What is the probability that it is the double-headed coin? Prior odds are 1 : 999; the likelihood ratio is 1 / (1/2)^{10} = 1024. Posterior odds are 1024 : 999, so the probability is 1024/2023 ≈ 0.506. The probability that the next flip is heads is then 0.506 × 1 + 0.494 × 0.5 ≈ 0.753.

### When the answer depends on how the information was obtained

The most-asked conditional probability puzzles are ones where the information you receive depends on a hidden process. The correct answer depends on that process, so state it.

**Monty Hall.** You pick one of three doors; one hides a car, two hide goats. The host, *who knows where the car is and always opens an unpicked door with a goat (choosing at random if both unpicked doors have goats)*, opens such a door and offers you a switch. Switching wins with probability 2/3: you win by switching exactly when your first pick was a goat, which happens with probability 2/3. If instead the host opens one of the other doors *at random* and it happens to show a goat, switching wins with probability 1/2. The difference is that in the second version, seeing a goat is evidence about your own door.

**Two children.** A family has two children, each independently a boy or a girl with probability 1/2. If you learn "at least one is a boy", the probability both are boys is 1/3 (the cases BB, BG, GB are equally likely). If you learn "the older one is a boy", it is 1/2. If you meet one of the children at random and it is a boy, it is again 1/2. A careful answer states which of these processes generated the information.

**Bertrand's box.** Three boxes contain two gold coins, two silver coins, and one of each. You pick a box at random and draw a coin at random from it; it is gold. The probability that the other coin in the box is gold is 2/3, not 1/2: of the three equally likely gold coins you could have drawn, two are in the gold–gold box.

## Random Variables and Expectation

A random variable assigns a number to each outcome. Its expectation is E[X] = Σ_{x} x P(X = x) (or ∫ x f(x) dx for a continuous variable): the long-run average value. Most interview questions ask for an expectation, and most of those are solved by one of three tools: linearity, first-step analysis, or the tail-sum formula.

### Linearity and indicator variables

**Linearity of expectation**: E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] and E[aX] = aE[X], *whether or not X and Y are independent.* This last clause is the source of its power. To find the expected number of times something happens, write the count as a sum of indicator variables (1 if the i-th event happens, 0 otherwise). The expectation of an indicator is the probability of its event, so

E[number of events that happen] = Σ_{i} P(event i).

**Worked example: fixed points.** In a random permutation of *n* items, how many items are expected to stay in their original position? Let I_{i} = 1 if item *i* stays put. P(I_{i} = 1) = 1/n, so the expectation is n × 1/n = 1, for every *n*. Computing the full distribution of fixed points is hard; the expectation took one line. (The indicators are not independent; linearity does not care.)

**Worked example: shared birthdays.** Among 23 people with independent, uniformly random birthdays over 365 days, the expected number of pairs sharing a birthday is C(23, 2)/365 = 253/365 ≈ 0.69. (Separately, the probability that *at least one* pair shares a birthday is about 0.507; computing that needs the complement 1 − (365 × 364 × ⋯ × 343)/365^{23}, not linearity.)

**Worked example: coupon collector.** How many rolls of a die until every face has appeared? Split the process into stages: after seeing *j* distinct faces, each roll shows a new face with probability (6 − j)/6, so the stage takes a geometric number of rolls with mean 6/(6 − j). By linearity, the total expectation is

6/6 + 6/5 + 6/4 + 6/3 + 6/2 + 6/1 = 6 × (1 + 1/2 + ⋯ + 1/6) = 6 × 49/20 = 14.7.

In general, collecting *n* equally likely coupons takes n H_{n} ≈ n (ln n + 0.577) draws on average, where H_{n} is the n-th harmonic number.

### The tail-sum formula

For a random variable taking non-negative integer values, E[X] = Σ_{k≥1} P(X ≥ k). It is useful for maxima and minima.

**Worked example: maximum of two dice.** P(max ≤ k) = (k/6)^{2}, so P(max ≥ k) = 1 − ((k − 1)/6)^{2}. Summing for k = 1 to 6 gives 6 − (0 + 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25)/36 = 6 − 55/36 = 161/36 ≈ 4.47.

### First-step analysis

When a process restarts, condition on what happens at the first step and use the fact that the remaining process looks like the original (this is the tower property E[X] = E[E[X | first step]]).

**Worked example: flips until HH.** Let E_{0} be the expected number of further flips when the last flip was not heads (or at the start), and E_{1} when the last flip was heads. Then

E_{0} = 1 + ½ E_{1} + ½ E_{0},  E_{1} = 1 + ½ × 0 + ½ E_{0}.

The first equation gives E_{0} = 2 + E_{1}; substituting the second gives E_{0} = 3 + ½ E_{0}, so E_{0} = **6**. The same method for HT gives **4**: once you have an H you only wait for a T (2 more flips on average), and getting the first H takes 2. Why is HH slower when both patterns have probability 1/4 at each position? Because a failed attempt at HH (an H followed by a T) throws away all progress, while a failed attempt at HT (H followed by H) keeps you one step in. Section 2.7 turns this into a general tool.

### Optimal stopping by backward induction

**Worked example: a dice game.** You roll a die and receive its face value in dollars, but you may reject the first roll once and take the second roll instead. Work backwards: the second roll is worth 3.5 on average, so keep the first roll if it exceeds 3.5, i.e. if it is 4, 5 or 6. The game is worth

P(keep) × E[face | keep] + P(re-roll) × 3.5 = ½ × 5 + ½ × 3.5 = 4.25.

With two re-rolls allowed, the continuation value after the first roll is 4.25, so keep only 5 or 6: (2/6) × 5.5 + (4/6) × 4.25 = 14/3 ≈ 4.67. The principle generalizes: *at each decision, compare the value of stopping with the expected value of continuing, computed from the end backwards.*

### Variance and covariance

Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])^{2}] = E[X^{2}] − (E[X])^{2}. For sums, Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y), where Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y]. Variance is additive only for uncorrelated variables (in particular, independent ones). For a sum of indicators, Var(Σ I_{i}) = Σ P(A_{i})(1 − P(A_{i})) + Σ_{i≠j} (P(A_{i} ∩ A_{j}) − P(A_{i})P(A_{j})); Problem P2.6 uses this.

### Expected value of guessing under negative marking

Chapter 1 promised this calculation. On a multiple-choice question worth +4 if right and −1 if wrong, with four options and no knowledge, a random guess is worth ¼ × 4 − ¾ × 1 = +0.25 marks: guess. Under +1/−⅓ marking with four options, a blind guess is worth ¼ − ¾ × ⅓ = 0, but eliminating even one option makes it ⅓ − ⅔ × ⅓ = +1/9: guess whenever you can eliminate one. The general rule: guess when P(correct) × gain > P(wrong) × penalty.

## Common Distributions

Recognizing a named distribution saves you from re-deriving its mean and variance. These are the ones that appear in QD rounds:

| Distribution | P(X = k) or density | Mean | Variance | Arises as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bernoulli(p) | P(1) = p, P(0) = 1 − p | p | p(1 − p) | one yes/no trial |
| Binomial(n, p) | C(n, k) p^{k} (1 − p)^{n−k} | np | np(1 − p) | number of successes in *n* independent trials |
| Geometric(p) (trials) | (1 − p)^{k−1} p, k ≥ 1 | 1/p | (1 − p)/p^{2} | trials up to and including the first success |
| Poisson(λ) | e^{−λ} λ^{k} / k! | λ | λ | count of rare independent events in a fixed window |
| Uniform(a, b) | 1/(b − a) on [a, b] | (a + b)/2 | (b − a)^{2}/12 | "pick a random point" |
| Exponential(λ) | λ e^{−λx}, x ≥ 0 | 1/λ | 1/λ^{2} | waiting time between Poisson events |
| Normal(μ, σ^{2}) | (2πσ^{2})^{−1/2} e^{−(x−μ)^{2}/2σ^{2}} | μ | σ^{2} | sums of many small independent effects |

### Properties you will be asked about

- **Two conventions for the geometric.** "Number of trials until the first success" has mean 1/p; "number of failures before the first success" has mean (1 − p)/p. Say which you mean.
- **Memorylessness.** The geometric (discrete) and exponential (continuous) distributions are the only memoryless ones: having already waited *s* tells you nothing about the remaining wait. P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t).
- **Poisson as a limit of the binomial.** If *n* is large and *p* small with np = λ, Binomial(n, p) is approximately Poisson(λ). The number of fixed points of a random permutation is approximately Poisson(1), which is another way to see that P(no fixed point) ≈ e^{−1}.
- **Sums and splitting of Poissons.** Independent Poisson(λ) and Poisson(μ) counts add to Poisson(λ + μ). If each event of a Poisson(λ) process is independently kept with probability *p*, the kept events form a Poisson(pλ) process.
- **Poisson processes.** If messages arrive independently at an average rate of 2 per millisecond, the number in a 1 ms window is Poisson(2), so P(no message in 1 ms) = e^{−2} ≈ 0.135, and the gaps between messages are Exponential with mean 0.5 ms. (Real market-data traffic is burstier than a Poisson process; this is a modelling exercise, not a fact about feeds.)
- **Competing exponentials.** If X_{i} ~ Exponential(λ_{i}) are independent, their minimum is Exponential(Σ λ_{i}), and the probability that X_{j} is the smallest is λ_{j} / Σ λ_{i}.
- **The normal approximation and the CLT.** A sum of many independent, identically distributed variables with finite variance is approximately normal (central limit theorem). About 68%, 95% and 99.7% of a normal distribution lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean.

**Worked example: sum of 100 dice.** One die has mean 3.5 and variance 35/12. The sum of 100 dice has mean 350 and standard deviation √(100 × 35/12) ≈ 17.1. The probability that the sum exceeds 380 is roughly P(Z > 30.5/17.1) = P(Z > 1.79) ≈ 0.04. (The 0.5 is a *continuity correction* for approximating a discrete variable by a continuous one.)

## Continuous and Geometric Probability

When a point is chosen uniformly in a region, probability is the ratio of areas (or lengths, or volumes). Draw the region.

**Worked example: meeting.** Two people each arrive at a uniformly random time between 12:00 and 13:00 and each waits 15 minutes for the other. In the unit square of arrival times (x, y), they meet when |x − y| ≤ ¼. The complement is two triangles with legs ¾, of total area (¾)^{2} = 9/16, so P(meet) = 7/16 ≈ 0.44.

**Worked example: broken stick.** A stick of length 1 is broken at two independent uniform points. The three pieces form a triangle exactly when every piece is shorter than ½ (triangle inequality). In the square of break points, that region has area ¼, so P(triangle) = **1/4**. Section 2.11 checks this by simulation.

**Worked example: distance between two uniforms.** For X, Y independent Uniform(0, 1), E|X − Y| = 1/3. (Compute ∫∫ |x − y| dx dy = 2 ∫_{0}^{1} ∫_{0}^{x} (x − y) dy dx = 2 ∫_{0}^{1} x^{2}/2 dx = 1/3.)

### Order statistics of uniforms

If U_{1}, …, U_{n} are independent Uniform(0, 1), the *n* points split [0, 1] into n + 1 gaps that are exchangeable, so each gap has expected length 1/(n + 1). Therefore the k-th smallest value has expectation k/(n + 1): the minimum is expected at 1/(n + 1) and the maximum at n/(n + 1). The same argument gives Problem P2.7 its one-line solution.

**Worked example: how many uniforms until the sum exceeds 1?** P(U_{1} + ⋯ + U_{n} ≤ 1) = 1/n! (the volume of a simplex). Let N be the number of uniforms needed for the sum to exceed 1. Then P(N > n) = 1/n!, and by the tail-sum formula E[N] = Σ_{n≥0} 1/n! = e ≈ 2.718.

### "Random" must be defined

Bertrand's paradox asks for the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Choosing two random endpoints on the circle gives 1/3; choosing a random distance from the centre along a random radius gives 1/2; choosing a random midpoint inside the disc gives 1/4. Each is a correct answer to a different question. In an interview, this is the cleanest illustration of why you state how "random" is generated.

## Markov Chains and First-Step Analysis

A **Markov chain** is a process that moves between states, where the probability of the next state depends only on the current state, not on the history. It is described by a transition matrix P, with P_{ij} the probability of moving from state *i* to state *j*.

### Absorbing chains: expected times and absorption probabilities

Many interview questions are "how long until X happens" or "which of X and Y happens first". Model them as a chain with **absorbing** states (states you never leave). For the transient states, first-step analysis gives one linear equation per state:

- expected time to absorption: t_{i} = 1 + Σ_{j transient} P_{ij} t_{j};
- probability of being absorbed in state *a*: h_{i} = P_{ia} + Σ_{j transient} P_{ij} h_{j}.

In matrix form, with Q the transient-to-transient block, t = (I − Q)^{−1} 1. For two or three states you solve by hand, as for HH above. For more, a program solves it (Listing 2.3).

### Waiting for a pattern

To wait for a pattern such as HTH, let the state be *the longest suffix of the flips so far that is a prefix of the pattern*. For HTH the states are ∅, H, HT, and HTH (absorbing). From HT, a T leads back to ∅ (no suffix of HTT is a prefix of HTH), but an H completes the pattern. From H, an H keeps you at H. This "longest suffix that is also a prefix" is exactly the failure function of the KMP string-matching algorithm (Section 4.7), and Listing 2.3 builds the chain that way.

For a fair coin there is also a shortcut worth memorizing. For each k = 1, …, L where the pattern's prefix of length *k* equals its suffix of length *k*, add 2^{k}. (For an alphabet of size *m* with equal probabilities, add m^{k}.)

| Pattern | Self-overlaps (k) | Expected waiting time |
|---|---|---|
| HH | 1, 2 | 2 + 4 = 6 |
| HT | 2 | 4 |
| HHH | 1, 2, 3 | 2 + 4 + 8 = 14 |
| HTH | 1, 3 | 2 + 8 = 10 |
| HHT | 3 | 8 |
| HTHT | 2, 4 | 4 + 16 = 20 |
| Two sixes on a die ("66") | 1, 2 | 6 + 36 = 42 |

This shortcut can be proved with a martingale argument (a "fair casino" in which gamblers bet on the pattern); for interviews it is enough to know it and to be able to verify small cases with first-step analysis. Listing 2.3 reproduces every row.

### Which pattern comes first?

Two players each pick a pattern of three coin flips; the coin is flipped until one pattern appears. Surprisingly, for every pattern the first player picks, the second player can pick one that wins with probability at least 2/3 (*Penney's game*). Two examples:

- **THH vs HHH.** HHH wins only if the first three flips are HHH (probability 1/8). Otherwise, a T has appeared before any HHH, and the first HHH must then be preceded by a T, so THH appears first. P(THH first) = 7/8.
- **HHT vs HTH.** P(HHT first) = 2/3, which Listing 2.3 computes exactly.

The relation "beats" is not transitive, which is why the game works. In an interview, the expected answer is a correct first-step analysis on the joint state (the longest suffix that is a prefix of *either* pattern).

### Stationary distributions

For a chain that runs forever, a **stationary distribution** π satisfies π = πP: if the chain starts in π it stays in π. For an irreducible, aperiodic chain on finitely many states, the distribution converges to π from any start, and π_{i} is the long-run fraction of time spent in state *i*. For two states with P(1 → 2) = a and P(2 → 1) = b, balancing the flows a π_{1} = b π_{2} gives π = (b/(a + b), a/(a + b)).

## Random Walks and Martingales

### Gambler's ruin

You have *k* dollars and bet 1 dollar on fair coin flips, stopping at 0 or *N*. Let p_{k} be the probability of reaching *N*. First-step analysis gives p_{k} = ½ p_{k+1} + ½ p_{k−1}, so p_{k} is linear in *k*; with p_{0} = 0 and p_{N} = 1, **p_{k} = k/N**. The expected number of bets, from t_{k} = 1 + ½ t_{k+1} + ½ t_{k−1} with t_{0} = t_{N} = 0, is **t_{k} = k(N − k)**. A walk started at 0 needs on average N^{2} steps to reach distance *N* in either direction.

For a biased game that you win with probability *p* (lose with q = 1 − p, p ≠ q), let r = q/p. Then

P(reach N before 0 | start at k) = (1 − r^{k}) / (1 − r^{N}).

Letting N → ∞: if the walk drifts upward (p > q), the probability of *ever* hitting 0 from *k* is r^{k} = (q/p)^{k}; if p ≤ q, it is 1.

**Worked example: the drunk near a cliff.** A man stands one step from a cliff edge. Each step, he moves toward the edge with probability 1/3 and away with probability 2/3. The probability he eventually falls is (1/3)/(2/3) = 1/2.

### Martingales and optional stopping

A **martingale** is a process whose expected next value, given the past, is its current value: a fair game. The symmetric walk S_{n} is a martingale. So is S_{n}^{2} − n, and, for a biased walk, (q/p)^{Sₙ}. The **optional stopping theorem** says that, under conditions that hold for these bounded examples, the expected value at a stopping time equals the starting value. That gives the ruin results in one line each:

- E[S_{T}] = k and S_{T} is either 0 or N, so N × P(reach N) = k.
- E[S_{T}² − T] = k^{2}, so N^{2} × (k/N) − E[T] = k^{2}, giving E[T] = k(N − k).

The same theorem kills every "doubling strategy" for beating a fair game: with a finite bankroll or a finite time limit, any stopping rule leaves the expected final wealth equal to the starting wealth.

### The reflection principle and the ballot problem

In an election, candidate A receives *a* votes and B receives *b* votes, with a > b, and the votes are counted in random order. The probability that A is strictly ahead throughout the entire count is **(a − b)/(a + b)**. The proof uses the reflection principle: paths that touch the tie line can be put in bijection with paths that start with a vote for B, by reflecting the part before the first tie. This is the counting idea behind Catalan numbers (C_{n} = C(2n, n)/(n + 1), the number of balanced bracket sequences of length 2n).

Two facts about the simple symmetric walk on the integers are often asked: it returns to its starting point with probability 1, but the expected time to return is infinite.

## Classic Puzzles and Brainteasers

The puzzles below are grouped by the method that solves them, because recognizing the method is the skill being tested. Try each before reading the solution.

### Symmetry and relabelling

**Ants on a stick.** 100 ants are placed on a 1-metre stick, each facing left or right, all walking at 1 m/min. When two ants meet, both reverse direction. Ants fall off the ends. What is the longest time before all ants have fallen off? *Method:* two ants bouncing off each other is indistinguishable from two ants passing through each other, if you ignore their labels. So each "ant position" simply walks straight to an end. Answer: at most 1 minute.

**Pólya's urn.** An urn starts with one red and one blue ball. Repeatedly draw a ball at random and return it together with another ball of the same colour. After *n* draws, the number of red balls is uniformly distributed on 1, …, n + 1, and the probability that the next draw is red is ½ at every step (before you look). *Method:* compute the probability of any specific sequence of colours; it depends only on how many reds it contains, not on their order.

### Working backwards

**Race to 100.** Two players alternately add a whole number from 1 to 10 to a running total that starts at 0; whoever makes the total exactly 100 wins. Who wins, and how? *Method:* find the losing positions for the player about to move, working backwards. If you hand your opponent a total of 89, any move they make lands in 90–99, from which you reach 100. So 89 is winning for the player who reaches it, and by the same argument so are 78, 67, …, 12, 1 (every number congruent to 1 modulo 11). The first player says 1 and then always completes the opponent's move to 11.

**The secretary problem.** *n* candidates arrive in random order; after each interview you must hire or reject immediately, and you want to hire the best. The optimal strategy for large *n* is to reject the first n/e candidates (about 37%), then hire the first candidate better than all seen so far. It succeeds with probability approaching 1/e ≈ 0.368.

### Invariants, parity, pigeonhole

**Mutilated chessboard.** Remove two opposite corners from an 8 × 8 board. Can the remaining 62 squares be covered by 31 dominoes? *Method:* invariant. Each domino covers one black and one white square. Opposite corners have the same colour, so 32 squares of one colour remain and 30 of the other. Impossible.

**100 light bulbs.** 100 bulbs start off. On pass *k* (k = 1, …, 100) you toggle every k-th bulb. Which bulbs end on? *Method:* bulb *n* is toggled once per divisor of *n*. Divisors come in pairs (d, n/d) except when d = n/d, so only perfect squares have an odd number of divisors. Answer: the 10 bulbs 1, 4, 9, …, 100.

**Pigeonhole.** Any 5 points in a unit square include two at distance at most √2/2 ≈ 0.707: split the square into four half-size squares; two points share one, and its diagonal is √2/2.

### Information and optimization

**Nine balls, one heavier.** With a balance scale, find the heavier ball in 2 weighings. *Method:* each weighing has 3 outcomes (left heavy, right heavy, balanced), so weigh 3 against 3: the outcome identifies the group of 3 containing the heavy ball; repeat with 1 against 1 within that group. The counting bound "k weighings distinguish at most 3^{k} cases" is the general principle (the 12-coin problem, where the odd coin may be heavier or lighter, has 24 cases ≤ 27, and 3 weighings suffice).

**Bridge and torch.** Four people need 1, 2, 5 and 10 minutes to cross a bridge; at most two cross at a time, at the slower one's pace, and the single torch must accompany every crossing. Minimum time: 17 minutes. 1 and 2 cross (2), 1 returns (1), 5 and 10 cross (10), 2 returns (2), 1 and 2 cross (2). *Method:* send the two slowest together so their times overlap.

**25 horses.** With 5 lanes and no timer, find the 3 fastest horses in the minimum number of races. Answer: 7. Five heats, then race the five heat winners (race 6). Only the top three from race 6 can include a top-three horse; the candidates for 2nd and 3rd overall are the 2nd and 3rd in race 6, the 2nd and 3rd in the heat of race 6's winner, and the 2nd in the heat of the race-6 runner-up: five horses, one final race.

**Ropes.** Each of two ropes burns in exactly 60 minutes, but unevenly. Measure 45 minutes. Light rope 1 at both ends and rope 2 at one end. Rope 1 finishes at 30 minutes; at that moment light the other end of rope 2, which then finishes 15 minutes later.

### Probability classics with surprising answers

**100 prisoners and boxes.** 100 numbered prisoners; 100 boxes contain their numbers in random order. Each prisoner may open 50 boxes, and all must find their own number, with no communication. Strategy: prisoner *i* opens box *i*, then the box whose number was found, and so on (following the cycle of the permutation). Everyone succeeds exactly when the permutation has no cycle longer than 50, which has probability 1 − Σ_{k=51..100} 1/k ≈ 0.312, compared with (1/2)^{100} for random guessing.

**The two-envelope paradox.** One envelope holds twice as much as the other; you open one and see *x*. The argument "the other holds 2x or x/2 with equal probability, so its expected value is 1.25x, so switch" is wrong because no proper prior distribution makes "the other is double" equally likely for every *x*. Switching without looking cannot change the expected value, by symmetry.

**St Petersburg paradox.** A game pays 2^{k} if the first head occurs on flip *k*. Its expected value is Σ (1/2)^{k} × 2^{k} = ∞, yet nobody would pay a large amount to play. The usual resolutions are a finite bankroll of the counterparty (which caps the payout and makes the expectation small) and concave utility. The interview point is that expected value alone is not a complete decision rule when variance is extreme.

## The Nanosecond Penalty

This subject's "performance" questions are about how fast you can generate random numbers and how many samples you need. Both matter in practice: simulation is used for backtesting, risk and testing of trading systems.

How to read these numbers:

- **Absolute values depend on hardware**, compiler version and system load, and a shared virtual machine adds noise. The *ratios* are the useful part, and the reasons for them are architectural, so they transfer.
- **`std::rand()` is roughly an order of magnitude slower** than the modern generators here. In glibc it is an out-of-line call into the library that acquires and releases an internal lock on every call (to be thread-safe), so even a single-threaded loop pays for atomic operations and a function call. Nothing can be inlined or vectorized.
- **`std::mt19937`** keeps 624 32-bit words of state (about 2.5 KB) and regenerates the whole array every 624 outputs, so most calls are cheap and some are expensive; the average is a few nanoseconds. Its state is too large to live in registers.
- **`std::minstd_rand`** has tiny state but computes a multiplication modulo 2^{31} − 1, which involves a 64-bit remainder; that is why a "simpler" generator is not faster here. Its statistical quality is also poor by modern standards.
- **xoshiro256**** does a few register operations per call, about one to two nanoseconds here, which is only a few cycles at this clock speed.
- **Range reduction costs extra.** `std::uniform_int_distribution` must avoid bias, which costs a little more than a multiply-shift; the difference is small next to the choice of generator.

If a backtest draws 10^{9} random numbers, 20 ns versus 1.5 ns per number is the difference between about 20 seconds and 1.5 seconds of pure generation time.

## Interview Questions

Short model answers follow each question. The aim is to show the expected *shape* of an answer: method first, then the result, then a check.

**Q1. How do you produce a fair coin flip from a biased coin with unknown P(heads) = p?**
Flip twice. HT means heads, TH means tails; on HH or TT, start again. HT and TH both have probability p(1 − p), so the output is fair. Each round succeeds with probability 2p(1 − p), so the expected number of flips is 2 / (2p(1 − p)) = 1/(p(1 − p)). This is von Neumann's trick.

**Q2. Generate a uniform integer from 1 to 7 using only a fair six-sided die.**
Roll twice to get one of 36 equally likely outcomes. Use 35 of them, five for each of the numbers 1–7, and re-roll on the 36th. Each round succeeds with probability 35/36, so the expected number of rolls is 2 × 36/35 ≈ 2.06. The same rejection idea is how unbiased range reduction works in code.

**Q3. Three points are chosen uniformly on a circle. What is the probability that the triangle they form contains the centre?**
The triangle contains the centre exactly when the three points do *not* all lie in some semicircle. For *n* points, P(all in a semicircle) = n/2^{n−1}: for each point, the probability that the other n − 1 lie in the semicircle starting at it and going clockwise is 1/2^{n−1}, and these n events are disjoint. For n = 3 that is 3/4, so the answer is 1/4. (Equivalently, the triangle is acute with probability 1/4.)

**Q4. A revolver has six chambers, two adjacent bullets, and the cylinder was spun once. The first trigger pull was on an empty chamber. Before the second pull, should you spin again?**
No. If you spin, P(empty) = 4/6 = 2/3. If you do not spin, the next chamber is one of the four that follow an empty chamber; exactly one of those four is followed by a bullet, so P(empty) = 3/4 > 2/3.

**Q5. What is the expected number of flips of a fair coin to get *n* heads in a row?**
2^{n+1} − 2. By the overlap rule of Section 2.7 it is 2 + 4 + ⋯ + 2^{n}. Check: n = 1 gives 2, n = 2 gives 6.

**Q6. A and B alternately flip a fair coin, A first; whoever flips heads first wins. What is P(A wins)?**
Let x = P(A wins). A wins immediately with probability ½; otherwise B is now in A's position, so x = ½ + ½ (1 − x), giving x = 2/3. Check: A has an advantage, and the answer is between ½ and 1.

**Q7. What fraction of the integers from 1 to 1000 are divisible by 3 or 5?**
Inclusion–exclusion: ⌊1000/3⌋ + ⌊1000/5⌋ − ⌊1000/15⌋ = 333 + 200 − 66 = 467, so 0.467.

**Q8. Is there a betting strategy that wins money on average in a fair game?**
No, with finite capital or a finite time limit. Your wealth is a martingale, and the optional stopping theorem says the expected wealth at any such stopping time equals your starting wealth. Doubling strategies win small amounts with high probability and lose large amounts with small probability, and the two cancel exactly in expectation.

**Q9. How many people are needed for the probability of a shared birthday to exceed ½?**
23 (ignoring leap years and assuming uniform birthdays). A good check: the expected number of shared pairs among 23 people is 253/365 ≈ 0.69, and P(no shared pair) ≈ e^{−0.69} ≈ 0.50, which is consistent.

**Q10. How would you check a probability answer you computed in an interview?**
Check the range (probability in [0, 1], expectation within the support). Test extreme parameters (p = 0 or 1, n = 1 or 2) where the answer is obvious. Use symmetry where it applies. Compare with a rough estimate. Given a computer, run a seeded simulation with a standard error, as in Listing 2.2, and confirm the exact answer lies within about 3 standard errors.

## Key Takeaways

- **Process:** restate, state assumptions, pick a method, compute with visible intermediate steps, sanity-check. Give an exact answer, then a decimal.
- **Counting:** product and sum rules, C(n, k), multinomials, stars and bars (C(n + k − 1, k − 1); positive: C(n − 1, k − 1)). "At least one" means use the complement. Derangement probability → 1/e.
- **Bayes:** posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. Don't confuse P(A | B) with P(B | A). In Monty Hall and two-child puzzles, the answer depends on how the information was generated; state it.
- **Expectation:** linearity works without independence; count with indicators. Tail sum E[X] = Σ P(X ≥ k). First-step analysis for anything that restarts. Optimal stopping by backward induction.
- **Results to recall:** E[flips to HH] = 6, HT = 4, n heads in a row = 2^{n+1} − 2; coupon collector n H_{n} (14.7 for a die); fixed points have mean 1; k-th of n uniforms has mean k/(n + 1); broken stick forms a triangle with probability 1/4; meeting problem 7/16; E[uniforms to exceed 1] = e.
- **Distributions:** binomial np, np(1 − p); geometric 1/p; Poisson mean = variance = λ; exponential memoryless, min of exponentials has the summed rate; 68/95/99.7 rule; CLT for sums.
- **Markov chains:** absorbing chains give linear equations; pattern waiting time = sum of m^{k} over self-overlaps; Penney's game is non-transitive.
- **Random walks:** fair ruin P = k/N, duration k(N − k); biased P(ever hit 0) = (q/p)^{k} when p > q; optional stopping kills betting systems; ballot (a − b)/(a + b).
- **Puzzles:** symmetry/relabelling, working backwards, invariants and parity, pigeonhole, information counting (3^{k} outcomes for k weighings).
- **Code:** seed explicitly, use a modern generator (xoshiro256** or mt19937_64), avoid `rand() % n`, report a standard error; standard error ∝ 1/√N, so each extra digit costs 100× the samples. Compute exactly when the states can be enumerated.
