## Chapter 15: Networking

### *P15.1

Each frame occupies its own length plus 20 byte-times (8 preamble and start delimiter, 12 inter-frame gap).

(a) (64 + 20) × 8 bits / 10 Gb/s = **67.2 ns**, so at most 1 / 67.2 ns = **14.88 million frames per second**.

(b) 14 (Ethernet) + 20 (IPv4) + 8 (UDP) + 100 + 4 (FCS) = **146 bytes**; on the wire (146 + 20) × 8 = 1328 bits: **132.8 ns** at 10 GbE and **53.12 ns** at 25 GbE.

(c) (1518 + 20) × 8 / 10 = **1230.4 ns**.

(d) The minimum frame is 64 bytes including FCS, and headers plus FCS take 46, so payloads under **18 bytes** are padded. A receiver must take the payload length from the UDP header, never from the frame length (Listing 15.5, lines 173–176). *Verified.*

### *P15.2

The MAC is 01:00:5e followed by the low 23 bits of the group: the second octet loses its top bit. 239.1.1.1, 224.129.1.1 (129 & 0x7f = 1) and 239.129.1.1 all map to **01:00:5e:01:01:01**. A group address has 28 variable bits and the MAC carries 23, so **2^{5} = 32 groups** share each MAC. A host that joins one of them makes its NIC accept all 32; frames for groups it did not join are discarded only by the IP layer, after costing an interrupt share, an skb and protocol processing. Feed operators and firms choose group addresses that differ in the low 23 bits for this reason. *Verified.*

### *P15.3

(a) UDP preserves datagram boundaries; TCP does not (a byte stream), so TCP protocols add a length prefix or delimiter. (b) TCP; UDP feeds add sequence numbers and reorder or drop late packets. (c) TCP detects loss below the application; UDP feeds detect it from sequence-number gaps and heartbeats. (d) Neither: TCP's recovery delays everything behind the lost segment (head-of-line blocking); UDP feeds recover through the redundant line or a separate retransmission or snapshot service while live data keeps flowing. (e) UDP with IP multicast; TCP is point-to-point. (f) TCP (receive window and congestion control); UDP has none, so a too-fast sender causes drops at the receiver (15.3), and protocols rely on the sender being rate-limited (exchanges also throttle order entry, 18.4).

### *P15.4

Trace with `hold_limit` = 2. A:P1 delivers 1–2 and A:P2 delivers 3 (next = 4). B:P1 is a duplicate. A:P4 (seq 6) is ahead: held, waited = 1. B:P2 is a duplicate, waited = 2. A:P5 is held, waited = 3 > 2: the hole is declared, **gap 4–5**, and the held P4 and P5 are delivered (6, 7, 8; next = 9). B:P3 (4–5) and B:P4 are now duplicates. A:P7 is held (hole 9–11), B:P5 is a duplicate, and the heartbeat (next = 13) is held as well, exceeding the limit: **gap 9–11**, then 12 is delivered.

(a) Delivered 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 12; gaps **4–5** and **9–11**.

(b) With `hold_limit` = 4, A:P5 brings waited to 3, still within the limit; B:P3 then arrives in order, delivers 4–5 and drains 6, 7, 8. Delivered 1–8 and 12; only gap **9–11** (at the heartbeat or the final flush).

The gap 4–5 in (a) is **spurious**: line B delivered those messages one packet later. The cost is a needless retransmission request (load on the exchange's recovery service, which may also rate-limit you), and, if the handler does not wait for the retransmission, two messages applied to the book out of order or not at all; if it does wait, a delay for every later message. Gap 9–11 is real: P6 was lost on both lines. *Verified* by replaying the arrivals through Listing 15.3 in the verification program.

### *P15.5

(a) 300,000/s × 0.004 s = **1,200 datagrams**.

(b) 1,200 × 1,280 B = **1,536,000 bytes** as reported by `getsockopt`. The kernel doubles the requested value, so pass **768,000** to `setsockopt(SO_RCVBUF)`, and raise `net.core.rmem_max` to at least 768,000 (or use `SO_RCVBUFFORCE` with `CAP_NET_ADMIN`). Add headroom for bursts above the "peak" and for larger datagrams; buffers are cheap compared with gaps.

(c) The queue shrinks at 1,000,000 − 300,000 = 700,000 datagrams per second, so it empties after 1,200 / 700,000 = **1.714 ms**. A datagram that arrived during the stall is up to about 4 ms old when read, so the stall shows up in latency percentiles for nearly 6 ms of traffic even though nothing was lost. *Verified.*

### *P15.6

One-way times: fibre 1,250 km × 1.47 / c = **6.129 ms**; microwave 1,020 km × 1.0003 / c = **3.403 ms**; the advantage is **2.726 ms one-way, 5.452 ms round trip**. The theoretical minimum (great circle at c) is **3.336 ms**, so the microwave route is within 2% of physics. Microwave gives up bandwidth (orders of magnitude less than fibre, so only the most valuable data is carried), and reliability: rain fade and atmospheric conditions cause outages and errors, so it is used alongside fibre, not instead of it. *Verified.*

### *P15.7

(a) Bytes 42–49 span beat 5 (bytes 40–47) and beat 6 (bytes 48–55), counting from 0. The field is complete at the end of the seventh beat: **7 × 6.4 = 44.8 ns** after the frame starts.

(b) 200 bytes at 0.8 ns per byte = **160 ns** for the frame alone (25 beats); the FCS check, DMA into memory and software noticing the descriptor all come after that.

(c) The design acts before the FCS has been checked, so it must handle a frame that turns out to be corrupt (by discarding derived state, or by ensuring that an action already taken can be cancelled). It must also handle messages that span packet boundaries or arrive out of sequence (gap handling, as in 15.2, in hardware), and must apply the same risk checks to every action (17.7). *Verified* (beats and times).

### *P15.8

(a) Each line loses 500,000 × 10^{−4} = 50 packets per second, covered unless the other line lost the same packet: 2 × 500,000 × 10^{−4} × (1 − 10^{−4}) ≈ **99.99 packets per second** recovered by arbitration.

(b) 500,000 × 10^{−8} = **0.005 true gaps per second**, one every **200 seconds**, which recovery must handle routinely.

(c) Correlated causes: both lines crossing the same switch, link, NIC, or receiving host, so a microburst or buffer overflow there drops both copies (remedy: physically separate paths, separate NICs or ports, and receive-side capacity for both lines); and the receiving host itself falling behind, since a full socket buffer or a stalled handler drops both lines' packets together (remedy: separate queues and cores per line, larger buffers, a faster consumer). The independence assumption in (b) is only as good as the separation.

### *P15.9

`FrameReader` keeps one buffer large enough for a maximal frame (2 + 4,096 bytes). `feed` appends as much of the chunk as fits, extracts every complete frame (calling the handler with a span into the buffer: no allocation), moves the incomplete tail to the front, and repeats until the chunk is consumed. A length above the protocol maximum is reported as corruption: on TCP you cannot resynchronize inside a stream, so the session is dropped and re-established. The test serializes 100,000 random messages, including empty ones, and feeds the stream in random chunk sizes from 1 byte up to 64 KiB.

### *P15.10

(a) t_{2} − t_{1} = 900 ns and t_{4} − t_{3} = 300 ns. Offset = (900 − 300)/2 = **300 ns** (the slave is ahead); mean path delay = (900 + 300)/2 = **600 ns**.

(b) Forward: t_{2} − t_{1} = delay_{ms} + offset, so the true offset = 900 − 700 = **200 ns**. The error is 300 − 200 = **100 ns**, half of the asymmetry (700 − 500 = 200 ns).

(c) The two equations t_{2} − t_{1} = d_{1} + θ and t_{4} − t_{3} = d_{2} − θ have three unknowns (d_{1}, d_{2}, θ); only d_{1} + d_{2} and d_{1} − d_{2} + 2θ are observable, so any asymmetry is indistinguishable from offset. In practice it is reduced by hardware timestamping at both ends, boundary or transparent clocks in every switch (which remove queueing delays, the main source of asymmetry), symmetric cabling, and calibrating known asymmetries (some PTP implementations accept a configured delay asymmetry). *Verified.*

### *P15.11

(a) (7 − 4,294,967,291) mod 2^{32} = **12 descriptors**, computed simply as unsigned 32-bit subtraction. The next is in slot 4,294,967,291 & 2047 = **2043**. *Verified.*

(b) Free-running indices make "empty" (producer = consumer) and "full" (producer − consumer = size) distinguishable without wasting a slot or keeping a separate count, and each side writes only its own index (14.5). The power-of-two size makes the slot an AND rather than a division, and keeps the arithmetic correct across the 2^{32} wrap, because 2^{32} is a multiple of the size.

(c) Every frame held while waiting for line B is a frame not returned to the fill ring. During a long run of line-A losses, the handler holds more and more frames; once all frames are held or queued, the fill ring is empty, the kernel (or NIC) has nowhere to put new packets, and it drops them, including line B's packets that would have filled the holes: a self-inflicted outage. Listing 15.3 copies held packets into its own fixed pool (lines 69–79) and resolves the oldest hole when the pool is full (line 41), so receive frames are always recycled immediately (Listing 15.5, line 180). Zero-copy designs that do hold frames must bound the number held well below the ring size and fall back to copying or declaring gaps.

### *P15.12

1. Join the incremental channel first and buffer every message (in a bounded ring) from the first one received, sequence number S_{0}.
2. Join the snapshot channel and wait for a complete snapshot (it may span several packets; discard incomplete ones) whose "last included sequence number" L satisfies **L ≥ S_{0} − 1**. Then every incremental message after L is either buffered or still to come.
3. Build the book from the snapshot, discard buffered messages with sequence ≤ L, and apply the buffered messages from L + 1 onwards, checking that they are contiguous. The book is valid from that point; leave the snapshot channel and process incrementals live.
4. If the snapshot is older (L < S_{0} − 1), messages L + 1 … S_{0} − 1 are missing: discard that snapshot and wait for the next one (snapshots are published periodically), or request the missing range from the retransmission service if it covers it.
5. If a gap appears in the buffered incrementals, the book cannot be completed from this snapshot: restart from step 2 with the next snapshot, or request the range.

Memory bound: the buffer must hold every message that arrives between joining and applying a usable snapshot, at worst two snapshot periods (one may be just missed) at the peak message rate, plus the snapshot transmission time. At 1 million messages per second, a 1-second snapshot cycle and 64-byte messages, that is about 2 × 10^{6} × 64 B = 128 MB; allocate it up front (7.7) and treat overflow as "restart synchronization". While unsynchronized, the instrument is marked stale and no orders are sent on it (17.7). Section 17.4 describes how real feeds structure these channels.
