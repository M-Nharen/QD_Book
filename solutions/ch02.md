## Chapter 2: Probability and Brainteasers

### *P2.1

Multinomial count: 11 letters with M × 1, I × 4, S × 4, P × 2, so 11! / (1! 4! 4! 2!) = 39,916,800 / 1152 = **34,650**. *Verified* by enumerating with `std::next_permutation`, which visits each distinct arrangement exactly once.

### *P2.2

The outcomes with at least one 3 number 36 − 25 = 11 (the complement: neither die shows 3, 5 × 5 outcomes). Among them, sum 7 happens for (3, 4) and (4, 3). The answer is **2/11 ≈ 0.182**. A common error is to answer 2/12 by counting (3, 3) twice.

### *P2.3

(a) Prior odds double-headed : fair are 1 : 1. The likelihood ratio of three heads is 1 / (1/2)^{3} = 8. Posterior odds are 8 : 1, so P(double-headed) = **8/9 ≈ 0.889**.

(b) By total probability, P(next is heads) = 8/9 × 1 + 1/9 × 1/2 = **17/18 ≈ 0.944**. *Verified.*

### *P2.4

(a) 47 × 53 = 50^{2} − 3^{2} = 2500 − 9 = **2491**.

(b) Binomial expansion: (1 − 0.03)^{10} ≈ 1 − 10(0.03) + 45(0.03)^{2} − 120(0.03)^{3} = 1 − 0.3 + 0.0405 − 0.00324 ≈ **0.737**. Alternatively, 10 ln(0.97) ≈ 10 × (−0.03 − 0.00045) = −0.3045, and e^{−0.3045} ≈ 0.737. (True value: 0.73742.)

### *P2.5

Let I_{f} indicate that face *f* appears at least once. P(I_{f} = 1) = 1 − (5/6)^{6} = 1 − 15625/46656 = 31031/46656. By linearity, the expected number of distinct faces is 6 × 31031/46656 = 31031/7776 ≈ **3.991**. *Verified.*

### *P2.6

Let X = Σ I_{i}, with I_{i} indicating that item *i* is fixed. E[I_{i}] = 1/n, so **E[X] = 1**.

For the variance, E[X^{2}] = Σ_{i} E[I_{i}^{2}] + Σ_{i≠j} E[I_{i} I_{j}]. Since I_{i}^{2} = I_{i}, the first sum is 1. For i ≠ j, P(both fixed) = (n − 2)!/n! = 1/(n(n − 1)), and there are n(n − 1) ordered pairs, so the second sum is 1. Thus E[X^{2}] = 2 and **Var(X) = 2 − 1 = 1** for every n ≥ 2. (Consistent with the Poisson(1) approximation, whose mean and variance are both 1.)

For n = 4, the number of derangements is D_{4} = 4! (1 − 1 + 1/2 − 1/6 + 1/24) = 24 − 24 + 12 − 4 + 1 = 9, so P(no fixed point) = 9/24 = **3/8**. *Verified* (simulation for the moments with n = 10, enumeration for n = 4).

### *P2.7

The 4 aces split the 48 other cards into 5 gaps (before the first ace, between aces, after the last). By symmetry the gaps have equal expected size, 48/5 = 9.6. The number of cards up to and including the first ace is the first gap plus 1: **53/5 = 10.6**. In general, with N cards of which *a* are special, the answer is (N + 1)/(a + 1). *Verified.*

### *P2.8

**HTH.** States: ∅ (no progress), H, HT.

E_{∅} = 1 + ½ E_{H} + ½ E_{∅};  E_{H} = 1 + ½ E_{H} + ½ E_{HT};  E_{HT} = 1 + ½ × 0 + ½ E_{∅}.

The first gives E_{∅} = 2 + E_{H}; the second gives E_{H} = 2 + E_{HT}; so E_{∅} = 4 + E_{HT} = 5 + ½ E_{∅}, and E_{∅} = **10**.

**HHT.** States: ∅, H, HH. From HH, a further H keeps you at HH, so E_{HH} = 2 (wait for a T). E_{H} = 1 + ½ E_{HH} + ½ E_{∅} = 2 + ½ E_{∅}, and E_{∅} = 2 + E_{H} = 4 + ½ E_{∅}, so E_{∅} = **8**.

**Why they differ.** Both patterns occur at the same long-run rate (1/8 per position), so the average gap between occurrences is 8 for both. But HTH overlaps itself (it can end with the H that starts the next HTH), so its occurrences come in clumps: some gaps are very short (2), which forces the other gaps, including the wait for the first occurrence, to be longer. HHT cannot overlap itself, so its occurrences are spread out. The overlap rule of Section 2.7 gives the same numbers: HTH → 2 + 8 = 10, HHT → 8. *Verified.*

### *P2.9

States: 0 (last roll was not a 6, or start) and 1 (last roll was a 6).

E_{0} = 1 + (1/6) E_{1} + (5/6) E_{0}, so E_{0} = 6 + E_{1};  E_{1} = 1 + (1/6) × 0 + (5/6) E_{0}.

Substituting, E_{0} = 7 + (5/6) E_{0}, so E_{0} = **42**. (Overlap rule: 6 + 36.) *Verified.*

### *P2.10

Fair gambler's ruin with k = 3, N = 10: P(reach 10) = k/N = **0.3**, and the expected number of bets is k(N − k) = 3 × 7 = **21**. *Verified.*

### *P2.11

Let V be the value of the game. Rolling again is worth V − 1, so V = E[max(D, V − 1)] for a die roll D. Try V = 4: E[max(D, 3)] = (3 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/6 = 24/6 = 4. So **V = 4**.

The solution is unique: the function g(V) = E[max(D, V − 1)] increases with slope P(D < V − 1) < 1, so g(V) = V has at most one solution. Optimal strategy: **keep 4, 5 or 6; re-roll 1 or 2; 3 is indifferent** (keeping 3 and re-rolling for a net 4 − 1 = 3 are worth the same). A direct check: "keep ≥ 4" gives V = ½ × 5 + ½ × (V − 1), so V = 4. *Verified* for both "keep ≥ 3" and "keep ≥ 4".

### *P2.12

Let h be the probability of *ever* moving down one level from the current position. First step: h = 0.4 + 0.6 h^{2} (either step down now, or step up and then need to come down two levels, each an independent copy of the same problem). The roots of 0.6h^{2} − h + 0.4 = 0 are h = 1 and h = 2/3. Because the walk drifts upward, the correct root is the smaller one (the probability is the minimal non-negative solution), h = 2/3 = q/p. Dropping three levels needs three independent such events: (2/3)^{3} = **8/27 ≈ 0.296**. *Verified* (the simulation stops walks that reach 60, from where the chance of returning is (2/3)^{60}, about 3 × 10^{−11}).

### *P2.13

Let the first break leave pieces X and 1 − X, and let L = max(X, 1 − X), which is uniform on [½, 1]. The short piece 1 − L is at most ½. Break the long piece at A, uniform on [0, L], into A and L − A. A triangle needs every piece below ½: the short piece always is, and we need A < ½ and L − A < ½, i.e. A ∈ (L − ½, ½), an interval of length 1 − L. So P(triangle | L) = (1 − L)/L. Averaging over L with density 2 on [½, 1]:

2 ∫_{1/2}^{1} (1 − L)/L dL = 2 [ln L − L]_{1/2}^{1} = 2 (−1 − (−ln 2 − ½)) = **2 ln 2 − 1 ≈ 0.386**.

It is larger than the 1/4 of the two-uniform-breaks problem, because breaking the longer piece avoids the worst case of an already-short piece. *Verified.*

### *P2.14

The condition is that no odd number appears before the first 6. Equivalently: keep rolling until the first roll in {1, 3, 5, 6}; the condition holds exactly when that roll is a 6. The rolls of 2 and 4 in between do not affect anything.

Let T be the number of rolls until the first result in {1, 3, 5, 6}. T is geometric with p = 4/6, and by symmetry the value that ends it is equally likely to be any of the four and independent of T. So conditioning on "it ended with a 6" does not change the distribution of T: **E[T | condition] = E[T] = 6/4 = 3/2**.

The tempting answer of 3 treats the problem as rolling a three-sided die {2, 4, 6}. That is a different question: it conditions each individual roll on being even, rather than conditioning the whole sequence, and the conditioning in the actual problem strongly favours short sequences. *Verified* by rejection sampling.

### *P2.15

**1/2.** At each step where a displaced passenger chooses randomly, seat 1 (the first passenger's seat) and seat 100 (the last passenger's seat) are equally likely to be chosen, and the first time either is taken settles the outcome: if seat 1 is taken, everyone else, including the last passenger, gets their own seat; if seat 100 is taken, the last passenger does not. Any other choice just passes the problem to another displaced passenger. Because the two decisive seats are symmetric at every such step, each is taken first with probability ½. The last passenger always ends up in seat 1 or seat 100. *Verified.*
